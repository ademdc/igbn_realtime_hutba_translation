In many mosques across Europe, the khutbah is delivered in Bosnian, Turkish, Arabic or Albanian. For the generation that grew up here, that is often a barrier: they understand a lot, but not everything. And it is usually the most important thoughts of a sermon that get lost.

## Why the language of the khutbah becomes a challenge

Children and young people often speak their parents' language at home, but they read and think in the language of the country they live in. Add converts, spouses from other countries and guests who do not speak the community's language at all. The khutbah is meant for everyone – but not everyone can follow it.

## Existing solutions and their limits

Mosques have tried different approaches:

- **A second, shorter khutbah in the local language.** It makes Friday prayer longer and asks the imam to prepare two sermons.
- **A printed summary.** It has to be written in advance, and anything the imam adds freely is missing.
- **An interpreter.** Rarely available, and hard to organise every Friday.

## How live translation works

With Hutba the imam speaks as always. A phone or laptop at the minbar picks up the sermon, and the congregation reads along sentence by sentence on their own phones – translated within a few seconds.

1. **The imam starts the broadcast** with one tap on "Start".
2. **The khutbah is translated in real time**, for example into English, German and Dutch.
3. **The congregation scans a QR code** on the poster at the entrance. No app, no sign-up.

Those who prefer not to use a phone can read along on a screen in the mosque.

## What mosques should keep in mind

- **The microphone matters most.** The closer the device is to the imam, the better the recognition. A simple clip-on microphone helps in large halls.
- **Speak clearly and in full sentences.** It helps the translation as much as the listeners.
- **Qur'anic verses in Arabic** are not always rendered word for word. Many imams briefly give the meaning in their own language.
- **Machine translation** conveys the meaning of the sermon, but it does not replace a carefully reviewed written translation.

## Who it is for

Any community where not everyone understands the language of the khutbah equally well – which is most mosques in Europe. Young people stay connected, new members feel welcome, and the imam reaches everyone with a single sermon.
